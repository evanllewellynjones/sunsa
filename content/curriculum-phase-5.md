*Design the picture before you paint it. Arrange shapes so the eye moves where you want, draw structures that sit correctly in space, and use atmosphere to push distance. Finish the studio exercises at the bottom before moving to Phase 6.*

## 5.1. Compositional Armatures & Focal Hierarchy

*An underlying structure that the viewer feels without seeing.*

**Core concepts**

- **Armatures:** rule of thirds, golden ratio, dynamic symmetry (diagonals and reciprocals of the rectangle), and classic shape designs — S-curve, L-design, steelyard (a large mass balanced by a small one far from center), radiating lines.
- **Focal hierarchy:** one area wins. Give it the most value contrast, the hardest edges, and the highest chroma; everything else plays a supporting role.
- **Eye path:** lead-in lines, overlapping shapes, and value steps carry the eye in and keep it circulating.
- **Tangents:** shapes that just touch — a tree kissing the frame edge, a roofline aligning with a hill — create visual tension. Overlap clearly or separate clearly.

## 5.2. Linear Perspective & Structural Drawing

*Getting the drawing right under the paint.*

**Core concepts**

- **Horizon line = eye level.** Everything above it you look up at; everything below, down on. Parallel lines receding from you meet at vanishing points on the horizon (1-point when facing a plane straight on, 2-point when looking at a corner). Inclined planes — roofs, streets going uphill — converge to points above or below the horizon.
- **Measuring:** sight-size (drawing at the size the subject appears from your position), comparative measurement with a pencil at arm's length, and plumb lines (true verticals) to check alignments.
- **Man-made in nature:** buildings, fences, and roads follow perspective strictly; trees and rocks don't, so build the structure first and let organic forms soften around it.

## 5.3. Aerial (Atmospheric) Perspective

*Air between you and the subject.*

**Core concepts**

- **What distance does:** values move toward the sky's value (darks lighten, lights darken slightly), contrast drops, edges soften, chroma falls, and temperature usually cools toward blue.
- **Receding planes:** knock greens back with their complement and a touch of the sky color rather than white alone — white by itself turns distance chalky.
- **Overlap and scale:** overlapping shapes and diminishing size reinforce the atmospheric effect. Design three clear planes: foreground, middle ground, background.

## Phase 5 Studio Exercises

### Exercise 1 · Four Armatures, One Subject

**Objective:** see how structure changes the same subject.

1. Pick one reference photo with a clear subject.
2. Make four 3"×4" value thumbnails, each re-composed on a different armature: rule of thirds, S-curve, L-design, steelyard.
3. In each, mark the focal point and draw an arrow for the eye path.
4. Check every thumbnail for tangents and fix them.

**What to observe:** which version feels most intentional. Use it for Exercise 4.

### Exercise 2 · Perspective Box Street

**Objective:** draw a convincing street scene from simple boxes.

1. On a 9"×12" sheet, draw a horizon line one-third from the bottom.
2. Place one vanishing point; draw a road and four boxes (buildings) receding to it.
3. On a second sheet, redraw the scene in 2-point perspective, turning the boxes to a corner view.
4. Add doors and windows that shrink correctly with distance.

**What to observe:** placing the horizon low makes buildings tower; high makes you look down on the scene.

### Exercise 3 · Five-Plane Atmospheric Strip

**Objective:** paint distance with value, chroma, and temperature only.

1. On a long panel (4"×12"), paint five overlapping hill silhouettes from foreground to far distance.
2. Foreground: darkest, warmest, highest chroma, hardest edge.
3. Each step back: lighter, cooler, grayer, softer — move toward the sky color.
4. The farthest hill should be just barely distinguishable from the sky.

**What to observe:** the strip reads as miles of depth with no detail at all.

### Exercise 4 · Designed Landscape

**Objective:** combine the phase.

1. Using your best thumbnail from Exercise 1, paint an 8"×10" landscape.
2. Hold the focal point to the highest contrast, hardest edges, and highest chroma.
3. Use the atmospheric steps from Exercise 3 for the background.
4. Squint at the finished piece: does the eye land where you planned?

**What to observe:** whether the value design survives the addition of color and detail.
