*Putting it all together on classic subjects. Every module here is value, color, edges, and composition applied to a particular kind of form. Finish the studio exercises at the bottom before moving to Phase 7.*

## 6.1. Skies, Clouds & Water

*The largest, simplest shapes — and the easiest to overwork.*

**Core concepts**

- **Gradated skies:** a clear sky is usually deepest and most saturated overhead (zenith) and lighter and warmer toward the horizon. Blend blue into a warm light (not pure yellow, which turns green) and keep plenty of paint on the surface so the blend doesn't streak.
- **Clouds:** paint them as three-dimensional forms — a lit top, a shadowed underside that's cooler and grayer, and bright rims (silver linings) where backlit. Vary edges: crisp on the light side, soft and wispy at the trailing edges.
- **Water:** still water reflects vertically below the object, usually a little darker than lights and a little lighter than darks. Moving water breaks reflections into horizontal strokes; shallows show the bottom and a lighter, warmer color.

## 6.2. Landscape Elements: Rocks, Mountains & Trees

*Big shapes first, detail last.*

**Core concepts**

- **Rocks & cliffs:** treat them as faceted blocks with a top plane, a side plane, and a shadow plane. Top planes catch sky light (cooler), sides catch sun (warmer). A palette knife edge gives crisp fracture lines.
- **Mountains:** apply Phase 5 atmospheric perspective — the farther the range, the closer its value and color to the sky.
- **Trees & foliage:** group leaves into a few large value masses (light, middle, shadow) before any leaf detail. Add sky-holes — darker and smaller than open sky, varied in size and placement — so the tree doesn't read as a lollipop. Vary greens by warming with yellow/orange and cooling with blue; avoid tube green straight.

**Video lessons**

## 6.3. Still Life, Florals & Material Textures

*Every material is a pattern of values and edges.*

**Core concepts**

- **Glass:** paint what you see through and around it — distorted background, a few sharp highlights, dark edges where the glass thickens. Less is more.
- **Metal & ceramics:** specular highlights are small, sharp, and very light; the rest of the object reflects its surroundings with high contrast.
- **Drapery:** folds are cylinders and cones. Find where the fabric hangs from (support points) and paint the big light-and-shadow pattern before the small folds.
- **Flowers:** treat each head as a simple sphere, cup, or cone in light first; individual petals follow that larger form.

## 6.4. Figure, Portrait & Anatomical Mass

*Structure first, likeness second.*

**Core concepts**

- **Planes of the head:** reduce the head to a few big planes — forehead front and sides, cheek planes, nose, jaw — and light them like the cube in Phase 2.
- **Figure block-in:** start from the gesture and large masses (rib cage, pelvis, limbs as cylinders); muscle groups come after.
- **Flesh tones:** a common pattern is three color zones down the face — forehead more yellow, cheeks/nose/ears redder, jaw and chin cooler (bluish/greenish in men especially). Shadows cool or warm depending on the light, as in Phase 3.

**Video lessons**

## Phase 6 Studio Exercises

### Exercise 1 · Three Skies

**Objective:** control gradients and cloud form.

1. Paint three 5"×7" skies: a clear gradient sky, a sky with one cumulus cloud, and an overcast sunset.
2. On the clear sky, blend zenith to horizon without streaks or green.
3. On the cumulus, give the cloud a lit top, cool gray underside, and a mix of hard and soft edges.
4. On the sunset, keep the brightest value near the horizon.

**What to observe:** the clouds look solid only when the underside is clearly darker than the lit top.

### Exercise 2 · Reflection Pair

**Objective:** still vs. moving water.

1. Paint the same simple scene twice (a post or a tree on a shoreline).
2. Version A: still water — reflect the object straight down, a touch darker.
3. Version B: rippled water — break the reflection into horizontal strokes with gaps of sky color.

**What to observe:** the horizontal breaks alone are enough to read as movement.

### Exercise 3 · Rock & Tree Value Masses

**Objective:** structure before detail.

1. Paint a boulder and a tree side by side on one 8"×10" panel.
2. Allow yourself exactly three values for each in the first pass.
3. Only after the masses read correctly, add sky-holes to the tree and fracture lines (knife) to the rock.

**What to observe:** if the three-value pass looks right, the detail pass is easy; if not, detail won't fix it.

### Exercise 4 · Material Still Life

**Objective:** three textures in one painting.

1. Set up a glass, a metal spoon, and a folded cloth under one light.
2. Paint it at 9"×12", starting with the big value pattern of the cloth.
3. Save the sharpest highlights and hardest edges for the glass and spoon.

**What to observe:** each material is identified mostly by its edges and highlight shape, not by detail.

### Exercise 5 · Planes-of-the-Head Study

**Objective:** light a head like a simple form.

1. Using a planar head reference (or a photo with strong side light), paint a monochrome head study in three values.
2. Then repaint it in color, adding the warm/cool face zones.

**What to observe:** likeness improves when the big planes are right, even with no features drawn carefully.
