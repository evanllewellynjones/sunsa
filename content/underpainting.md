An underpainting and toned ground are the structural backbone of an acrylic painting. They move you away from the anxiety of a stark white canvas and give you immediate control over composition, light and color harmony.

### 1. Toned ground (imprimatura)

A **toned ground** is a thin, translucent wash of neutral color applied over the gessoed panel or canvas *before* you begin painting.

- **Eliminating white glare:** pure white canvas throws off your judgment — against it, darks look darker and mid-tones look richer than they really are. A mid-tone ground (warm **Burnt Sienna**, **Raw Umber** or a neutral grey) lets you judge true values from the start
- **Unified harmonies:** letting peeks of the ground show through thin washes or brush gaps creates an atmospheric tie that unifies the whole palette
- **Color temperature interaction:**
  - **Warm ground** (earth tones, ochres) → gives a glowing, luminous warmth under cool overpaints (blues, greens)
  - **Cool ground** (cool greys, muted blues) → stark, crisp structural contrast for architecture or cold mountain granite

### 2. Underpainting (verdaccio, grisaille or monochrome)

An **underpainting** is a fully developed monochrome or limited-palette block-in of the composition. It does the heavy lifting of **value** before any local color goes on.

- **Value map & compositional anchor:** separating value (light and shadow) from hue (color) makes sure the tonal composition reads from a distance. If the underpainting works in a single color, the final color painting is very likely to work too
- **Built-in luminosity for glazing:** acrylics dry slightly darker and satin-matte, which makes value adjustments tricky. Strong lights and deep darks in the underpainting become the foundation for transparent [glazes](topic.html?t=glazing) — light bounces back through the glaze off the contrast below, giving deep optical richness
- **Overcoming acrylic dry time:** because acrylics dry fast, the underpainting lets you map hard [edges](topic.html?t=edges), focal points and shadow boundaries without panicking over wet-in-wet blending. Lock in the structural drawing *before* committing to complex color mixing

### Recommended execution for acrylics

1. **Seal & prime** — seal the wood panel or canvas with a quality acrylic size or primer, then **2–3 coats of gesso**, sanded smooth
2. **Apply the ground** — mix a fluid wash of an earth pigment (**Burnt Umber** or **Raw Sienna**) with acrylic glazing liquid or water. Brush broadly over the surface, then **wipe back** the excess with a soft rag to leave an even, glowing mid-tone. Let it dry fully
3. **Establish the underpainting** — with a thin, fast-drying mix, block in the **deep shadow masses first**. Then pull out the mid-tones, and **reserve the brightest highlights** — leave them as the toned ground, or add a crisp white touch later
