## 2.1. The Value Scale & Tonal Compression

*Seeing and mixing value independent of color.*

**Core concepts**

- **The 9-step scale:** black (1) to white (9) in even visual steps. Mixing one by hand trains your eye to judge value far better than any app.
- **Tonal compression:** paint can't match the range of real light — the brightest white on canvas is far dimmer than sunlight. Painters compress the scene's range into paint's narrower one, choosing where to spend contrast.
- **Squint and simplify:** squinting kills detail and merges values, revealing the 3–4 big masses that carry the picture. Notan (two-value) and three-value thumbnails lock in the design before paint.

## 2.2. Toned Grounds & Imprimatura

*Killing the white and starting from the middle.*

**Core concepts**

- **Why tone:** a white canvas makes every early value read too dark. A mid-value ground lets you judge lights and darks against a neutral middle from the first stroke, and stray gaps of ground unify the painting.
- **Ground temperature:** warm earths (Burnt Sienna, Raw Umber, Yellow Ochre) glow through cool passages — classic for landscapes and portraits. Cool or neutral grays suit warm-dominant subjects or high-key work.
- **Wipe-out (subtractive) technique:** lay a thin transparent wash, then lift the lights out with a rag or brush while it's still open. In acrylic, work fast or add retarder — once it dries it won't lift.

## 2.3. Monochromatic Underpainting (Grisaille & Verdaccio)

*Building form in one color before committing to local color.*

**Core concepts**

- **Grisaille** (gray) and **verdaccio** (muted green-gray, traditional for flesh) are one-color underpaintings that settle drawing and value so later color layers only have to deal with hue and chroma.
- **Darks first:** block in the shadow shapes as simple masses. Get the big light/shadow split right before anything else.
- **Light logic:** every lit form moves through highlight → light mass → halftone → core shadow → reflected light → cast shadow. Reflected light stays darker than any value in the light.
- **Check before color:** photograph the underpainting in grayscale and compare to the reference. Fix value errors now — color won't save them.

## Phase 2 Studio Exercises

### Exercise 1 · Paint a 9-Step Value Scale

**Objective:** mix even value steps by eye — the single most useful drill in painting.

1. Rule nine 1-inch squares in a row on a primed board.
2. Paint square 1 Mars Black (or a chromatic black) and square 9 Titanium White.
3. Mix square 5 as the exact visual midpoint. Then 3 (between 1 and 5) and 7 (between 5 and 9). Then fill 2, 4, 6, 8.
4. Let it dry — acrylic darkens — and repaint any step that shifted.
5. Punch a hole in the center of each square, or cut a strip with holes, to make a value-checker you can hold up to a scene.

**What to observe:** whether each step reads as one equal jump. Most first attempts bunch up in the darks.

### Exercise 2 · Three-Value Thumbnails

**Objective:** design with value masses before painting.

1. Choose 3 reference photos (a landscape, a still life, a street scene).
2. For each, draw a 3"×4" box and paint it using only three values: dark, middle, light (Raw Umber + white works well).
3. Combine anything closer than one value apart. No detail, no lines.
4. Repeat one of them as a strict two-value notan in black and white.

**What to observe:** which thumbnail still reads clearly at arm's length. That's the strongest composition.

### Exercise 3 · Toned-Ground Wipe-Out

**Objective:** map values subtractively on a warm ground.

1. Tone an 8"×10" panel with a thin Burnt Sienna or Raw Umber wash mixed with retarder or open medium.
2. While wet, use a rag, cotton swab, and stiff brush to lift out the lights of a simple subject (an egg or a mug under one lamp).
3. Add darks with the same pigment, unthinned.
4. Work until the ground starts to grab — note how long that took.

**What to observe:** how quickly a convincing light-and-shadow structure appears from only lifting and adding one color.

### Exercise 4 · Grisaille Sphere-and-Cube Study

**Objective:** render the six light zones on simple forms.

1. Set a white ball and a white box under a single strong lamp.
2. Using only black, white, and your value scale from Exercise 1, block in the shadow shapes first.
3. Develop light mass, halftone, core shadow, reflected light, and cast shadow on each.
4. Keep reflected light darker than the halftones.

**What to observe:** the core shadow — the darkest band on the turning form — is what sells the volume.
