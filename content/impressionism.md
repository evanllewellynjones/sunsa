Impressionism is about **light changing over time** — capturing the feeling of a fleeting moment with visible, broken color rather than smooth, studio-finished detail. The first half below covers the movement's core principles; the second translates them into acrylic landscape practice.

## Core principles

### 1. En plein air — painting outdoors

Before the Impressionists, most paintings were meticulously finished inside dark studios using pre-mixed earth tones. The invention of **collapsible tin paint tubes in the 1840s** let artists step outside (*en plein air*) and capture nature directly.

- **The technique:** work quickly under the open sky to catch fleeting weather, light and atmosphere before the sun changes position

### 2. Broken color & short, rapid strokes

Impressionists abandoned smooth, blended transitions (the traditional *sfumato* approach) in favor of visible, textured brushwork.

- **The technique:** apply paint in short, thick dabs, dashes and commas — often with heavy [impasto](topic.html?t=impasto). Instead of mixing thoroughly on the palette, place **pure, unblended colors side by side** on the canvas
- **Optical mixing:** from a distance the viewer's eye blends adjacent strokes, creating a vibrant, shimmering effect flat pre-mixed color can't match

### 3. The science of light & shadow

[Shadows](topic.html?t=shadows) are rarely black, gray or brown — they're filled with reflections from surrounding light and objects.

- **The technique:** paint shadows with rich **blues, purples and greens**; capture highlights with **warm yellows, oranges and pure whites** to suggest the intense quality of direct sunlight

### 4. Rejection of black & earth tones

Traditional academic palettes leaned heavily on bitumen, raw umber and black for dark values.

- **The technique:** narrow the palette to bright, prismatic colors (cobalt blue, cadmium red, chrome yellow, viridian, flake white). Black was largely eliminated

### 5. The transient moment

Inspired by early photography and Japanese woodblock prints (*ukiyo-e*), Impressionists sought a single, unposed split-second in time.

- **The technique:** cropped edges, asymmetrical framing and blurred figures in motion — prioritizing the *feeling* of a bustling boulevard, a shimmering lily pond or a smoky railway station over rigid academic geometry

## Applying it with acrylics

Impressionist techniques were developed for oil, which stays wet and blendable for days. Acrylics dry fast, so capturing shimmering light, broken color and spontaneous energy — outdoors or in the studio — needs specific strategies.

### 1. Managing drying time

Impressionism relies on **wet-into-wet** (*alla prima*) work and optical mixing on the canvas. Acrylics dry fast to a permanent finish, so manage working time:

- **Stay-wet palette** — a damp sponge under palette paper keeps paint workable for hours
- **Water mister** — lightly mist palette and canvas to keep [edges](topic.html?t=edges) soft and workable
- **Retarder / slow-drying mediums** — extend drying time so you can drag and feather strokes much like oils

### 2. Broken color & visible brushwork

Build form from distinct, choppy strokes instead of smooth gradients.

- Load a **filbert or bright** with unblended or semi-mixed paint
- Apply **short dabs, dashes and commas**. Let strokes sit proudly — don't overwork them
- **Optical blending:** place contrasting or complementary colors side by side (e.g. warm yellow dabs next to cool lavender). From a few feet away the eye mixes them into a glow a muddy pre-mix can't match

### 3. Impasto & palette knives

Impressionists — and later Post-Impressionists like Van Gogh — used thick paint so the texture itself catches light. See the [Impasto](topic.html?t=impasto) page for the full method.

- Use **heavy-body acrylics** straight from the tube, or mix with **impasto gel** for sculptural body without losing adhesion
- Use a **metal palette knife** for skies, water highlights or rocky foregrounds — thick ridges create physical highlights that catch the light

### 4. Banish black & embrace luminous shadows

Shadows are full of reflected light and [color](topic.html?t=color), not absence of light.

- **No tube black:** mix chromatic darks from deep complements — **Ultramarine Blue + Burnt Umber** or **Viridian + Alizarin Crimson**
- **Color-shift shadows:** paint tree trunks, path shadows and mountain crevices with deep purples, blues and rich greens
- **Luminous highlights:** opaque, high-chroma strokes (**Naples Yellow, Titanium White, Cadmium Lemon**) for direct sun on leaves, water and rooftops

### 5. Light & atmospheric perspective

- **Work background → foreground:** block in sky and distant mountains first with cooler, desaturated blues and soft grays; build forward through mid-ground foliage; finish with high-contrast, heavily textured foreground strokes
- **Hasty notation:** skip microscopic detail (individual leaves, blades of grass). Use sweeping, directional strokes for wind through a meadow or light dancing on water

## Suggested acrylic starter palette

| Group | Colors |
|---|---|
| Whites / Yellows | Titanium White, Naples Yellow, Cadmium Yellow Medium |
| Reds / Oranges | Cadmium Red Light, Quinacridone Magenta (or Alizarin Crimson) |
| Blues / Greens | Ultramarine Blue, Cobalt Blue, Viridian Green, Sap Green |
| Earths | Raw Umber, Burnt Sienna, Yellow Ochre |

*Earths here are for mixing chromatic darks and toning, not for muddying lights — keep them out of the sunlit passages.*
