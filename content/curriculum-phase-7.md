*Knowing when a painting is done, rescuing one that isn't, and protecting the finished work for decades.*

## 7.1. Self-Critique & Problem Solving

*Diagnose before you repaint.*

**Core concepts**

- **A diagnosis checklist** for a stalled painting: (1) Does it read in grayscale? Photograph it and desaturate. (2) Is there one clear focal point, or several competing? (3) Are the values too compressed (everything mid-gray) or too jumpy? (4) Are transitions muddy from overmixing? (5) Are all the edges the same hardness?
- **Fresh eyes:** turn the painting upside down, view it in a mirror, or step back 10 feet. Errors in drawing and value jump out.
- **Acrylic rescue:** once cured, acrylic can be scraped back with a blade, sanded (220–400 grit) to remove texture, or isolated with a thin coat of gloss medium so you can repaint an area cleanly and wipe off a new attempt without disturbing what's under it.

## 7.2. Isolation Coats & Varnishing

*The last layer protects all the others.*

**Core concepts**

- **Isolation coat:** a clear, permanent (non-removable) acrylic layer — typically soft gel gloss thinned with water — applied over the cured painting. It seals the absorbent matte areas so the varnish goes on evenly, and protects the paint if the varnish is ever removed for cleaning.
- **Varnish types:** solvent-based (e.g., Golden MSA, removable with mineral spirits) vs. waterborne polymer (removable with ammonia solution). Both come in gloss, satin, and matte; matte is often mixed with gloss to avoid a hazy look in darks. Choose one with UV protection.
- **Application:** paint fully cured (days to weeks for thick impasto), dust-free room, thin coats, one direction per coat, and let it dry flat.

## Phase 7 Studio Exercises

### Exercise 1 · Rescue an Old Painting

**Objective:** practice the diagnosis checklist on real work.

1. Pick a painting you're unhappy with.
2. Photograph it in grayscale; write down answers to all five checklist questions.
3. Choose the single biggest problem and fix only that.
4. Re-photograph and compare.

**What to observe:** one targeted fix usually helps more than a general repaint.

### Exercise 2 · Isolate-and-Try

**Objective:** use an isolation layer as a safety net.

1. On a cured painting (or a practice board), brush a thin coat of gloss medium over one area. Let it dry.
2. Repaint that area with a bold change — new color, new value, new edge.
3. If you don't like it, wipe it off with a damp rag before it dries, or sand lightly once dry.

**What to observe:** how freely you experiment when the original is protected.

### Exercise 3 · Sheen Test Board

**Objective:** choose your preferred finish before varnishing real work.

1. Paint a board with a dark passage, a mid-value passage, and a light passage, including some matte and some glossy areas. Let it cure.
2. Apply an isolation coat over the whole board.
3. Mask it into four strips and varnish each differently: gloss, satin, matte, and a 1:1 gloss/matte mix.

**What to observe:** how each sheen affects the depth of the darks and the glare in the lights. Matte can grey down darks noticeably.

### Exercise 4 · Finish a Painting Start to End

**Objective:** close the curriculum with a fully finished, archival piece.

1. Choose your best painting from Phases 4–6.
2. Run the critique checklist and make final corrections.
3. Let it cure, apply an isolation coat, then varnish with your choice from Exercise 3.
4. Sign, date, and record the materials used (support, paints, mediums, varnish) on the back.

**What to observe:** whether you can call it finished and leave it — that's the real skill.
