*The chemistry and physical behavior of acrylics, before worrying about composition. Finish the four studio exercises at the bottom before moving to Phase 2.*

## 1.1. Paint Grade, Viscosity & Rheology

*Chemical makeup, consistency variants, and label codes — before painting.*

**Core concepts**

- **Heavy body** (buttery, holds knife marks and peaks) vs. **fluid** (smooth, self-leveling, staining) vs. **high flow / inks** vs. **acrylic gouache** (ultra-matte, flat finish, no reflection).
- **Binder behavior:** acrylic polymer emulsions dry by evaporation and coalescence. The milky-white emulsion clears as water leaves, which is why colors visibly darken from wet to dry (the color shift).
- **Pigment load**, single pigments vs. multi-pigment "hue" convenience mixes, and ASTM lightfastness ratings.

**Video lessons**

- [Fluids vs Heavy Body Acrylics: Thick vs Thin](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8QHirx9uLLM) — flow differences, leveling behavior, and how different viscosities mix together.
- [How to Read Acrylic Paint Labels](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BytW2gak6kg) — ASTM ratings, pigment codes (e.g., PB29, PR108), series numbers, and transparency squares.

## 1.2. Supports & Surface Preparation

*Building stable substrates; controlling absorbency and tooth.*

**Core concepts**

- **Flexible vs. rigid:** stretched canvas and linen spring under the brush; cradled birch, hardboard, and gesso boards stay stable and take aggressive impasto and scraping.
- **Gesso application:** raw vs. acrylic-primed, cross-directional brushing, and fine-grit block sanding (220 to 400 grit) to set the mechanical tooth.
- **Sealing wood:** a coat of gloss medium blocks water-soluble wood tannins from bleeding into white or light layers — Support Induced Discoloration (SID).

**Video lessons**

- [Canvas vs Panels: Which Is Better for Painting?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2Q30Tna0Zqc) — rigid supports vs. stretched fabric: brush response, durability, knife techniques.
- [How to Prep Wooden Cradled Panels Before Painting](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bj4EhLYZ0iU) — sizing/sealing raw wood against tannins, then smooth gesso coats with sanding between.

## 1.3. Moisture Management & Open Time

*Drying rates, film integrity, and brush mechanics.*

**Core concepts**

- **Under-binding:** thinning standard acrylics with too much water (beyond roughly 30–50% by volume) breaks up the polymer film — chalking, flaking, or poor adhesion. Thin with medium instead.
- **Extending open time:** stay-wet palettes (sponge + permeable membrane), fine-mist spray atomizers, and retarder vs. blending/open mediums.
- **Brushes and tools:** synthetic filberts, flats, and mottlers (they hold up to high-pH acrylic binders), plus stainless-steel palette knives for clean mixing.

**Video lessons**

- [Better Blending with Acrylics](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1h1Hp73DCyk) — water vs. retarders vs. blending mediums, and the thinning limits that prevent under-binding.
- [How to Use a Sta-Wet Palette](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tm99cil9GTk) — sponge hydration, membrane prep, and keeping paint workable across days without over-diluting.

## Phase 1 Studio Exercises

Four physical drills to complete before value and underpainting in Phase 2.

### Exercise 1 · Wet-to-Dry Color Shift & Opacity Matrix

**Objective:** see how much acrylics darken as they dry, and check the opacity symbols on your tubes.

1. On a heavyweight strip of acrylic paper or primed board, paint a thick, solid black stripe down the center. Let it dry.
2. Choose 5 colors spanning different values (e.g., Titanium White, Yellow Ochre, Cadmium Red, Ultramarine Blue, Burnt Umber).
3. Paint a 2-inch horizontal swatch of each color across the black stripe.
4. Let the swatches dry fully.
5. Dab fresh, wet paint of the same color onto the left half of each dried swatch.

**What to observe:** compare each wet dab against the dried swatch right away — that gap is the tone shift. Then note which pigments hide the black stripe entirely (opaque) and which let it show through (transparent / semi-transparent).

### Exercise 2 · Rigid Panel Prep & Sanded Tooth Comparison

**Objective:** seal wood against tannins, and compare high-tooth vs. glass-smooth surfaces.

1. Take two small raw wood or MDF panels (roughly 6"×8" or 8"×8").
2. Seal both with two thin coats of acrylic gloss medium to stop tannin bleed. Let dry.
3. **Panel A:** apply 3 coats of gesso with a stiff brush, alternating direction each coat (horizontal, vertical, diagonal). Don't sand — this builds a textured, linen-like tooth.
4. **Panel B:** apply 3 thin coats of gesso thinned with about 10% water. Sand between dried coats with 320-grit, finishing with 400-grit for a marble-smooth surface.
5. Pull a single continuous stroke of heavy-body paint across each panel with a flat brush.

**What to observe:** drag, edge definition, and how the brush skips across Panel A's texture but lays down flat, even ribbons on Panel B.

### Exercise 3 · Dilution & Under-Binding Stress Test

**Objective:** find the physical limit of water dilution vs. acrylic medium.

1. Prepare 3 small swatches on a gessoed surface using a dark heavy-body pigment (Raw Umber or Phthalo Blue).
2. **Swatch 1:** thin with a little water (no more than 1:1).
3. **Swatch 2:** thin heavily with water (1 part paint to 8–10 parts water) until it behaves like watercolor.
4. **Swatch 3:** thin to the same wash consistency, using fluid gloss or matte medium instead of water.
5. Let all three cure for 24 hours.
6. Press a strip of low-tack artist's masking tape firmly across each swatch and peel it back quickly.

**What to observe:** Swatch 2 will likely lift or leave powdery pigment on the tape — the binder was too dilute to form a continuous film (under-binding). Swatch 3 stays put.

### Exercise 4 · Open-Time Blending & Retarder Control

**Objective:** learn acrylic's working window without letting the film turn gummy.

1. On a primed surface, blend a 6-inch smooth gradient from Titanium White into Ultramarine Blue using only paint and water from an open plastic palette. Time how many seconds you get before the paint starts to pull and tear.
2. Set up a wet palette and repeat the gradient with paint taken from it.
3. Repeat a third time, mixing about 15% slow-drying blending medium or retarder into both colors.

**What to observe:** the moment the paint turns tacky — the point where working it further damages the film. Note the working time each setup gives you in your room's humidity.
