## Brown & grey — the neutral modifiers

**Role of brown in tone** — Brown is a powerful direct tone modifier. Added to a pure hue it keeps the base color's identity (green stays green) while reducing saturation and warmth, creating naturalistic, earthy color. Essential for controlling color intensity.

**Creating browns** — Brown is a "dirty" mixture of many or all primaries, often made by mixing complements (like red and green) or several adjacent tertiaries, resulting in earth tones. Pigments like umber and sienna are natural sources.

**Role of grey in value** — Grey is the primary value modifier (tint and shade with intermediate value). Achromatic grey changes value and saturation without a temperature shift. Chromatic grey gives precise value control with a built-in color cast — crucial for rendering form and depth.

**Creating & using greys** — Greys are made achromatically (black + white) or chromatically (complementary pairs). Their main role is to desaturate hues: achromatic grey affects value most purely; chromatic grey adds subtle temperature complexity. Used to define shadows, form, and to harmonize strong hues.

## Complementary contrast

Complementary colors sit directly opposite each other on the wheel. Side by side they create **simultaneous contrast** — each makes the other appear brighter, more saturated and more vibrant.

### Blue & Orange — *strongest temperature contrast*

- **Effect:** the most dramatic warm-vs-cool pairing. Blue recedes and feels cool and atmospheric; orange advances and feels warm and luminous.
- **Pigments:** Ultramarine Blue + Cadmium Orange (or Burnt Sienna for an earthier contrast) · Phthalo Blue + Cadmium Orange (extreme saturation, electric energy)
- **Use:** sunlit skies against warm terrain, golden-hour light against cool shadows, autumn foliage against clear skies.

### Yellow & Violet — *strongest value contrast*

- **Effect:** pure yellow is the lightest pure hue, pure purple the darkest — color contrast *and* powerful light/dark separation.
- **Pigments:** Cadmium Yellow Light + Dioxazine Purple · Yellow Ochre + Ultramarine Violet (understated stone/earth relationship)
- **Use:** sunlit focal highlights (trusses, sunbeams) against deep interior shadow; pale flowers against rich shaded earth.

### Red & Green — *maximum hue separation*

- **Effect:** at full saturation both sit at mid values, so the contrast is purely chromatic. Matched in value they can "vibrate" along the edge.
- **Pigments:** Cadmium Red + Phthalo Green · Alizarin Crimson / Quinacridone Magenta + Sap Green (organic, forest feel)
- **Use:** accents inside dense landscapes — terracotta pots, red flowers, warm brick nestled in heavy green foliage.

### More pairs

- **Cyan & Red** — Phthalo Blue / Turquoise against Cadmium Red Light. Punchy, modern, graphic.
- **Magenta & Green** — Quinacridone Magenta against Viridian or Phthalo Green. Electric; high-impact floral and garden accents.
- **Yellow & Deep Blue** — Hansa Yellow against Indanthrone Blue.

### Managing high-contrast pairs

- **Avoid the 50/50 battle** — equal amounts at full saturation fight for attention and tire the eye. Use **80/20**: one color dominates (80% cool blues and greens), its complement is a targeted focal spark (20% warm ochres, golds, oranges).
- **Shift values to prevent vibration** — if a saturated red and green share the same lightness, the edge flickers. Lighten one (pale pink/peach) or darken the other (deep pine green).
- **Use them for neutral shadows** — mixed physically on the palette, complements cancel into rich, harmonious chromatic grays and deep shadows.

## Color harmony

Harmonious schemes rely on colors that share underlying bases. Rather than tension, harmony creates cohesion, unity and a soothing, natural balance.

### Analogous — neighboring colors

Three or four colors adjacent on the wheel; they share a parent color and flow together without friction.

- **Warm Earth & Sun** (yellow → yellow-orange → orange) — radiates sunlight and golden-hour warmth. *Cadmium Yellow Medium + Yellow Ochre + Raw Sienna + Cadmium Orange.* Use for sunlit path masonry, weathered stone walls, warm timber highlights.
- **Living Verdant** (yellow-green → green → blue-green) — lush, healthy growth and dense canopies. *Lemon Yellow (or Cad Yellow Light) + Sap Green + Viridian (or Phthalo Green) + Cobalt Turquoise.* Use for foreground garden beds, sunny leaves transitioning into cooler background shrubs.
- **Atmospheric Distance & Water** (blue-green → blue → blue-violet) — cool shadows, aerial recession, tranquility, moisture. *Cerulean + Ultramarine + Dioxazine Purple (sparingly).* Use for receding forest depths, shadowed roof slates, reflective pool water.

### Monochromatic — one hue, many values

A single hue varied through tints (white), shades (a deep chromatic dark) and tones (gray or an earth neutral).

- **Neutral masonry & stone** — base Yellow Ochre (or Raw Umber). Raw Umber (deep crevices) → Raw Sienna (mid-tone body) → Yellow Ochre + Unbleached Titanium (lit planes) → Titanium White + Buff (glints). Solid unity and tactile realism without looking busy.
- **Foliage massing** — base Sap Green. Sap Green + Burnt Umber (core shadows) → pure Sap Green (local color) → Sap Green + Cad Yellow / White (sunlit leaves). Big canopies read as one sculptural mass.

### Split-complementary — balanced contrast

One base color plus the two colors either side of its complement: the excitement of contrast with the cohesion of harmony.

- **Earthy foliage accent** — Sap Green base with Burnt Sienna / Terracotta (red-orange) and Alizarin Crimson / Quinacridone Magenta (red-violet). Keeps pots, wood tones and flowers from clashing with dominant foliage.
- **Warm light & cool shade** — warm ochre/orange stonework with cool teal foliage and soft violet-slate shadows. Clean outdoor sunlight without a harsh blue-orange battle.

### Triadic — evenly spaced

Three colors evenly spaced on the wheel. For landscapes and architecture, use earth or muted versions rather than pure primaries.

- **The Earth Triad** — Venetian Red, Yellow Ochre, Terre Verte / Olive Green. The classic Old Master palette: muted, grounded, inherently cohesive. Stone courtyards, aged timber, garden foliage, urns.

### Rules of thumb for color harmony

- **The "mother color"** — mix a pinhead of one dominant color (Raw Umber, Yellow Ochre or Ultramarine) into *every* mixture. Shared DNA across the canvas guarantees harmony.
- **60 · 30 · 10 hierarchy** — 60% dominant hue sets the mood (foliage greens); 30% analogous neighbor supports structure (ochre stone, warm timber); 10% accent draws the focal point (golden urn, crisp white gable).
- **Bridge with neutralized tones** — between analogous zones (sunny yellow-greens to cool slate blues), bridge with low-saturation earths (Raw Umber, Burnt Sienna, grayed ochre) to avoid jarring breaks.
